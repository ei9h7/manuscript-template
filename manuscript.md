## 1|Starting with a Blank Page|Why every book begins as a plain text file
Every book I have finished started the same way: a file with nothing in it, a blinking cursor, and the quiet suspicion that the cursor knew something I didn’t.
People expect a book to begin with a grand idea. Mine never do. They begin with a sentence that is mostly wrong, followed by another sentence that is slightly less wrong, and then — if the coffee holds out — a paragraph that sounds almost like a person talking. The grand idea shows up later, usually in chapter four, and asks why you didn’t save it a seat.
I keep the whole thing in plain text for one reason: plain text will outlive every app I have ever loved. Word processors come and go. File formats get retired, then forgotten, then turned into trivia questions. A text file from thirty years ago still opens on any computer you can find, and it will open on the ones that haven’t been invented yet.
[!callout] Tip: One line is one paragraph. You never have to think about styles, spacing or page breaks while you write — that is the layout’s job, not yours.
There is a second reason, and it is less practical. A plain file is honest. It can’t make a weak sentence look strong with a nice font. It can’t hide a dull chapter behind a pretty heading. When the words sit there unadorned, you see them the way a reader eventually will: as words, doing their work or failing to.
So the file stays plain. The *book* comes later.
I write each chapter as a single run of paragraphs. A heading line starts the chapter, and everything after it belongs to that chapter until the next heading arrives. If I want a word in italics, I wrap it in asterisks. If a paragraph deserves to stand apart — a rule, a warning, a recipe for disaster — I tag it as a callout and let the layout put it in a box.
That is the entire system. It is small on purpose. Every feature I don’t need is one fewer thing to break at two in the morning, the night before the proof is due.
[!callout] The blank-page rule: Don’t wait for the right first sentence. Write the wrong one and let the second sentence fix it.

## 2|The Long Way Round: Notes on Revision, Patience, and Other Unglamorous Virtues|What happens between the first draft and the proof
The first draft is a conversation with yourself. Revision is the moment you realise someone else will be listening.
Nobody warns you how much of a book is revision. You picture the writer at the desk, words pouring out, the story arriving fully formed. What actually happens is closer to furniture restoration: you sand, you look, you sand some more, and every so often you discover that a drawer was put in upside down three chapters ago.
I revise in passes. The first pass is for structure — does each chapter earn its place, and are they in the right order? The second is for sentences. The third is for the small, fussy, absolutely necessary things: the straight quotes that should be curly, the double hyphens that should be dashes, the three periods that should be a proper ellipsis…
[!callout] Check before you build: The app lists straight quotes, stray double hyphens and unmatched asterisks with their line numbers, and fixes the typographic ones for you in the output.
Then there are the compound words, which deserve their own support group. A once-in-a-generation storm. A state-of-the-art-but-somehow-still-broken printer. A do-it-yourself-and-regret-it-later weekend project. Words like these are a typesetter’s nightmare, because they refuse to fit anywhere and refuse to be broken anywhere sensible.
A good line breaker splits them where they already have hyphens, never in the middle of a syllable, and never so that a lonely fragment hangs at the end of a line. A careless one produces a proof with a word cut in half and no hyphen at all, and you only notice when the printed copy arrives and falls open to that exact page.
Patience is the other unglamorous virtue. Some chapters take a week. Some take a season. One chapter in my last book took two years, mostly because I kept writing it about the wrong thing. When I finally understood what it was about, it took an afternoon.
That is the long way round. There isn’t a short one.
[!callout] Revision rule: If a paragraph is hard to fix, try deleting it and reading what’s left. Half the time, nobody misses it.

## 3|Proof Day|Holding the thing you made
The proof arrives in a padded envelope that weighs exactly as much as a year of your life.
You open it slowly. You don’t mean to — your hands just decide that this is a moment for slowness. The cover is the first surprise: it’s real. Not a picture of a book on a screen, but a book, with edges and a spine and that faint smell of fresh ink and glue.
Then you open it, and the second surprise follows: the pages look like a book. The chapter opens on the right-hand page, the way chapters should. The heading sits where your eye expects it. The page numbers are tucked into the outside corners, out of the way, waiting to be useful.
And then you start reading, and you find the mistakes. There are always mistakes. A repeated word. A paragraph that ends one line too soon, leaving a single word stranded at the top of a page like the last guest at a party. A callout split across two pages, half its advice on one side and the punchline on the other.
[!callout] Proof checklist: Chapters open on the right. No single lines stranded at the top or bottom of a page. Callouts in one piece. Page numbers clear of the text.
The good news is that every one of those problems lives in the layout, not in your writing. Fix the setting, build again, and the book rearranges itself. Your sentences don’t have to change at all.
So you mark the proof, you fix the file, you build a new one, and a week later another padded envelope arrives. It weighs the same. It feels lighter.
That’s the whole process. Plain text in, book out — and somewhere in the middle, a person who wrote it.
